Family sessions are some of the most rewarding work a photographer can do, and some of the most unpredictable. A toddler who was delightful in the car park decides the studio is not for them. A newborn wakes hungry ten minutes in. A teenager would rather be anywhere else. At Desk and Studio, we have watched a lot of photographers turn those moments into the frames the family ends up printing, and the difference almost always comes down to preparation and to the room itself.

If you are thinking about moving your family work indoors, or you already shoot families and want a space that works harder for you, here is how to hire a studio for these sessions and get the most out of the day.

## Why a studio suits family work better than you might think

Parks are lovely, and a golden hour session on the grass will always have a place. But outdoor family shoots put you at the mercy of weather, harsh midday sun, wind in everyone's hair, and a two week reschedule when Sydney decides to rain.

A studio gives you certainty. You know exactly what the light will do. You know the session will go ahead. You know there is a toilet, a place to park the pram, somewhere to put the nappy bag, and heating in July. For parents booking a shoot around nap times and school pick up, that reliability is worth a great deal, and it is a genuinely good reason to charge confidently for a studio session.

There is a creative argument too. A clean, simple background puts the focus entirely on faces and on the way a family physically relates to each other. Nothing competes. Ten years later, that is the photograph that still works.

## What to look for when you hire the space

Family sessions ask slightly different things of a studio than a commercial shoot does. When you are comparing spaces, check for these.

1. **Room to move, and room to stand back.** Families spread out. A group of five needs width, and photographing children often means shooting from further away with a longer lens so they can play rather than perform. Ask for the shooting floor dimensions rather than just the overall size.
2. **Genuine natural light.** Soft window light is forgiving, flattering and completely silent. No flash means no startled babies, no recycle time, and no waiting while you chimp at a histogram. It also lets you shoot continuously through a moment instead of one frame at a time.
3. **A safe, clean floor.** Crawling babies and barefoot toddlers will be on that floor. Check that it is clean, that cables can be taped or routed away, and that light stands can be sandbagged well clear of small hands.
4. **A private space for feeding and changing.** A green room with a door means a parent can feed a baby, settle a meltdown or change a nappy without the whole session grinding to a halt. It is the single most appreciated feature on a newborn or toddler shoot.
5. **Warmth.** Newborn work in particular happens with very little clothing on. A room you can warm up properly is not optional.
6. **Somewhere to wait.** Siblings who are not being photographed need somewhere to sit that is not the middle of your set. Seating just off the shooting floor keeps the session calm.

## Planning the session so it runs smoothly

1. **Book the right time of day.** For babies and toddlers, mid morning after the first nap and a feed is almost always the sweet spot. Late afternoon sessions with young children are a gamble. Ask the parents what time their child is happiest and build the booking around that answer.
2. **Keep it short.** Forty five minutes to an hour of actual shooting is plenty for most families with young children. You will get your best frames in the first twenty minutes. Booking a two hour studio slot for a one hour session simply gives you a relaxed setup and a relaxed pack down.
3. **Brief the family before they arrive.** Send a short note covering what to wear, what time to arrive, where to park, and a gentle request to feed the children beforehand. Suggest coordinated rather than matching colours, and steer them away from large logos and busy prints.
4. **Set up before anyone walks in.** Lights positioned, backdrop rolled down, exposure tested on a stand in, music on softly. The moment the family arrives, your attention should be entirely on them.
5. **Have a plan for the first five minutes.** Let the children explore the space rather than expecting them to sit down and smile immediately. Photograph the parents together while the kids run around. By the time you turn the camera on the children, the room feels familiar.
6. **Work through a simple sequence.** Whole family, parents with each child, siblings together, then each child alone. Getting the group shot early means you have it before anyone tires.
7. **Leave time for the unposed frames.** A tickle, a whispered secret, a child climbing into a lap. These are usually the images that get ordered, and they only happen once everyone has stopped concentrating on being photographed.

## Lighting that keeps things simple

You do not need a complicated setup for family work. You need a setup you can forget about.

* **One large soft source is enough.** A big softbox or a window with a diffusion panel, placed to one side and slightly above, gives you soft shadows and lovely catchlights.
* **Add a reflector on the shadow side.** A white bounce fills the darker side gently and keeps faces open, which suits children and older family members alike.
* **Feather the light rather than moving people.** Angle the modifier so its edge falls across a group and you get even coverage without asking a four year old to hit a mark.
* **Give yourself depth of field to work with.** Children move. Shooting at f/1.4 with three people in frame will cost you more frames than it wins. Somewhere around f/4 to f/5.6 keeps everyone sharp.
* **Keep the shutter speed up.** At least 1/250 with a moving toddler, faster if you can.
* **Choose a forgiving backdrop.** Soft grey, warm neutral or clean white all work. Avoid anything with a strong pattern that will date.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should I book the studio for a family session?**<br />Allow around two hours for a one hour shoot. That gives you time to set up before the family arrives, shoot without watching the clock, and pack down properly afterwards. Newborn sessions often want longer, because feeding and settling take as long as they take.

**What is the best time of day for a shoot with young children?**<br />Mid morning, after the first nap and a feed, is usually the calmest window. Ask the parents, because every child is different, and build the booking around their answer rather than around your own diary.

**Do I need studio lights for family photography?**<br />Not necessarily. A studio with good natural light will carry most family and newborn sessions beautifully, and no flash means nothing startles a baby. Strobes give you more control on grey days and for larger groups, and you can hire what you need rather than buying it.

**Is a hired studio suitable for newborn photography?**<br />Yes, provided the room can be warmed properly and there is a private space for feeding and changing. Those two things matter far more than the size of the shooting floor.

**What should families wear?**<br />Coordinated tones rather than identical outfits, minimal logos, and no busy patterns. Suggest they bring a second outfit if they want variety, and remind them that bare feet photograph better than scuffed shoes.

**How much does it cost to hire a studio for a family shoot in Sydney?**<br />It depends on your hours and whether you add equipment. Our breakdown of [studio hire costs](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-hire-a-photography-studio-sydney/) will help you price your sessions with confidence.
